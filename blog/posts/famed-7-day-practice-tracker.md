If your study notes keep growing but you rarely repeat a difficult conversation, try tracking **what changed between two attempts**. This seven-day plan gives each session one task and one visible check. It is an independent practice suggestion, not an official exam timetable or a prediction of your result.

You need a fictional case or the organiser's practice material, a notebook and, if useful, a recording device or study partner. Keep patient-identifying information out of your practice notes and recordings. If you have only 15 minutes, shorten the task rather than skipping the second attempt.

## Set up one row for each session

Copy this tracker into your own notes. A tick means you completed the activity; it is not an exam score. Use a description such as “kept the three-day symptom duration” instead of “got better”.

| Day | Focus | First attempt saved? | One correction | Second attempt checked? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Establish a starting point | □ | … | □ |
| 2 | Anamnese: clarify and summarise | □ | … | □ |
| 3 | Aufklärung: explain supplied information | □ | … | □ |
| 4 | Arzt–Arzt: separate two cases | □ | … | □ |
| 5 | Writing: preserve the source facts | □ | … | □ |
| 6 | Repeat your hardest passage | □ | … | □ |
| 7 | Review and choose next week's task | □ | … | □ |

Use the [30-minute daily routine](/blog/famed-vorbereitung-30-minuten-lernroutine) to divide a session between preparation, practice, review and repetition. These are your study blocks, not the official station durations.

## Day 1: save a useful starting point

Choose one short task: an interview opening, a procedure explanation, a patient presentation or a written paragraph. Save your first attempt before editing it. Compare it with the supplied information and pick one problem you can actually check: an omitted time, an unclear question or an unsupported finding.

Write: “On my second attempt I will …”. Keep the original attempt so that your comparison has a real starting point. You do not need to assign yourself a percentage.

## Day 2: Anamnese — ask, listen and clarify

Practise an open question, one follow-up and a summary based on the answer. With a partner, let the answer guide your next question rather than reciting a fixed list.

> Was meinen Sie genau mit „Schwindel“?
>
> Ich fasse kurz zusammen: Die Beschwerden bestehen seit drei Tagen. Habe ich Sie richtig verstanden?

The second sentence is appropriate only if the fictional source actually says three days. On your repeat attempt, check whether you preserved the answer and gave the speaker room to correct you. Continue with the [Anamnese overview](/blog/anamnese-overview-part-1).

## Day 3: Aufklärung — explain without adding facts

Use the information supplied for the chosen procedure: its purpose, sequence, stated risks and instructions. Explain a short part in ordinary German, then invite a question.

> Welche Fragen haben Sie bisher dazu?

If practising with a partner, ask them to describe what they understood. Check any missing or distorted information against the source. Do not invent risks, preparation rules or reassuring promises to make the explanation sound complete. The [Aufklärung practice guide](/blog/famed-aufklaerungsgespraech-guide) gives a fuller structure.

## Day 4: Arzt–Arzt — keep two cases separate

Use two separate fictional case sheets. Present a brief opening for each and answer one follow-up from the supplied facts. Keep names, times and plans on their own sheet.

> Dazu liegen mir in den vorliegenden Angaben keine Informationen vor.

This is useful when a fact is genuinely missing, not when it is present and you overlooked it. Compare both recordings with their respective sheets. Repeat the passage in which you mixed or omitted information. [SBAR can organise practice](/blog/sbar-schema-arzt-arzt-gespraech); it is not a mandatory official answer template.

## Day 5: writing — preserve what the patient said

For exam-format practice, use the organiser's patient video and the actual writing instructions. For a shorter language drill, this original fictional statement is enough:

> „Seit drei Tagen habe ich Bauchschmerzen. Mir ist übel, aber ich habe nicht erbrochen.“

Write connected sentences that preserve the duration, symptom and explicit denial. The statement does not supply examination findings or a diagnosis. Underline those three source facts in your draft, then correct one language issue without changing their meaning. See the [written anamnesis exercise](/blog/famed-arztbrief-soap-schema).

## Day 6: repeat the difficult part, not everything

Choose the passage that caused the clearest problem this week. Record or write it again without looking at your previous wording. Compare it with the same source before comparing the language. Mark whether your chosen correction survived this fresh attempt. If it did not, make the passage shorter and repeat it once more.

## Day 7: decide what to practise next

Review your saved attempts. Which correction now holds up? Which difficulty keeps returning? Choose one task for next week and a concrete check for it. Completing seven rows shows that you practised; it does not establish exam readiness.

When moving to full timed tasks, use the organiser's current instructions, including reading and video time. The official human-medicine format includes patient communication, colleague presentations and written anamnesis.
